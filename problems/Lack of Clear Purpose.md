Many DAOs lack clear articulation of their purpose, values, and decision-making principles. Without a shared understanding of what the organization exists to do, governance becomes aimless — every proposal is evaluated in a vacuum rather than against coherent strategic direction. Despite having the lowest urgency score, this problem generated the most diverse set of proposed solutions, suggesting that practitioners see many possible paths forward.

## Evidence

**Breadth**: 15 out of 52 interviewees raised this problem. The moderate breadth indicates this is recognized across a meaningful cross-section of governance practitioners, though it may be overshadowed by more acute operational problems like token voting failure or governance theater.

**Misalignment of interests** (Tino, SEEDGov): Protocol teams want growth, DAOs want efficiency, token holders want price appreciation, and delegates want compensation — with no mechanism to converge these separate goals into a shared north star. Founders and CEOs blame DAOs for protocol failures while DAOs blame external factors, creating a cycle of mutual blame that stems from never having defined shared purpose in the first place.

**Depth**: Averaging 2.7 messages per discussion, the moderate depth suggests participants could identify the problem clearly but found it difficult to elaborate — perhaps because the absence of purpose is easier to name than to analyze. The problem is definitional rather than mechanical: it is about what is missing rather than what is broken.

## Why It Matters

Purpose gives governance its direction and evaluation criteria. Without it, governance devolves into politics — competing interests with no shared framework for resolution. Organizations that cannot articulate why they exist will struggle to make coherent decisions about how to operate. Purpose is the foundation that makes all other governance functions meaningful: voting, delegation, treasury allocation, and contributor management all require a shared understanding of organizational goals to function well.

## Proposed Solutions

- [[Signals Protocol]] — Creates structured mechanisms for communities to express and discover shared values and priorities, building purpose from the bottom up
- [[Co-creation Cycles]] — Collaborative proposal development processes that force communities to articulate and align on goals before jumping to solutions
- [[Tensegrity Governance]] — Governance architecture that encodes purpose into structural roles and relationships, making organizational mission a load-bearing element of governance design
- [[Specialized Committees]] — Domain-specific governance bodies that develop clear mandates, creating purpose at the working group level even when organizational purpose remains diffuse
- [[Proto-DAOs]] — Small-scale governance experiments that allow communities to discover and test their purpose before committing to permanent structures
- [[Centralized Ops DAO Oversight]] — Professional operational teams with clear mandates, creating purposeful execution even when broader community purpose is still forming
- [[Platform Cooperatives]] — Cooperative structures that come with built-in purpose frameworks (member benefit, democratic control), providing purpose as a structural feature rather than an aspirational add-on
- [[Governable Spaces]] — Framework that makes purpose articulation the first step in governance design, drawing on community organizing traditions for collective purpose-finding

## Related Problems

- [[Governance Theater]] — 5 participants raised both problems, connecting the absence of purpose to the prevalence of theater. Without clear goals, governance processes have no meaningful outcomes to produce — so they produce performances instead.
- [[Token Voting Failure]] — 4 participants raised both problems, observing that token voting asks "what should we do?" without first establishing "what are we trying to achieve?" — a sequence failure that makes voting outcomes arbitrary rather than strategic.
- [[Voting Fatigue]] — 4 participants raised both problems, noting that purposeless governance generates proposals without strategic coherence, creating an overwhelming volume of disconnected decisions that exhaust participants.
