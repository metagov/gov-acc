Collaborative design processes where proposals are developed iteratively with community input before reaching a vote. Co-creation cycles transform governance from ratification of top-down proposals into genuine collaborative design, addressing the root cause of governance theater: communities voting on proposals they had no hand in shaping.

## How It Works

A co-creation cycle replaces the linear propose-discuss-vote pipeline with an iterative loop: draft → community feedback → revision → more feedback → refined proposal → vote. The key difference from traditional "request for comments" periods is structure and commitment. In a co-creation cycle, the iteration process has defined stages, clear feedback mechanisms, and explicit commitments from proposal authors to incorporate community input.

Typically, a cycle begins with a problem statement rather than a solution. The community first aligns on what problem needs solving, then collectively explores solution space before a formal proposal is drafted. This prevents the common failure mode where a core team designs a solution in isolation and then asks the community to rubber-stamp it.

Each iteration narrows the design space based on community input. Early rounds explore broadly — what are the constraints, who is affected, what are the tradeoffs? Later rounds focus on specifics — implementation details, parameter choices, risk mitigations. By the time a proposal reaches a formal vote, it has already been shaped by community intelligence and reflects genuine collaborative design.

Some implementations include "temperature checks" between iterations — informal polls that gauge whether the proposal is converging toward something the community would support or whether fundamental disagreements remain that need to be addressed before proceeding.

## Problems Addressed

- [[Governance Theater]] — Directly counters rubber-stamp governance by ensuring communities shape proposals before voting on them, making participation meaningful rather than performative
- [[Lack of Clear Purpose]] — The initial problem-definition phase helps organizations articulate what they're actually trying to achieve before jumping to solutions
- [[Token Voting Failure]] — Reduces the stakes of any individual vote by ensuring proposals have been refined through community input, meaning the vote reflects genuine alignment rather than forced choice between flawed options

## Key Actors

- **Scroll DAO** — Implementing co-creation cycles as a core part of their governance process, with structured feedback rounds and community design workshops
- **NEAR** — Using iterative community design processes for protocol governance decisions, with particular attention to including diverse stakeholder voices in the design phase

## Maturity Assessment

At the Custom-to-Product boundary (ex 3), co-creation cycles are being actively used by several major governance organizations but haven't fully standardized. The core challenge is time — iterative design is slower than top-down proposal creation, and organizations facing urgent decisions often shortcut the process. Successfully implemented co-creation cycles require facilitation expertise and process design skills that are still rare in the web3 governance space.

## Participant Mentions

Referenced in 4 out of 50 interviews. Participants who mentioned co-creation cycles were typically experienced governance practitioners who had seen the failure mode firsthand: proposals designed by insiders that pass technically but lack community buy-in. They recognized that better governance outcomes require earlier community involvement, not just better voting mechanisms at the end.
