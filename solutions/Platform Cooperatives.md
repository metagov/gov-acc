Platforms structured as public infrastructure with cooperative governance, including algorithmic transparency mandates and stakeholder representation. Platform Cooperatives draw from the cooperative movement to provide an alternative to both corporate and DAO governance models — one that prioritizes public benefit and democratic stakeholder control.

## How It Works

Platform cooperatives apply the principles of cooperative ownership — democratic control, equitable participation, and community benefit — to digital platforms. Unlike traditional platform companies (where shareholders control governance) or typical DAOs (where token holders control governance), platform cooperatives distribute governance power among all stakeholders: users, workers, developers, and the broader public.

The governance structure typically includes multiple stakeholder classes with different representation mechanisms. Users might elect representatives to a user council. Workers (contributors, operators) might have direct governance participation through a worker council. The broader public interest might be represented by independent directors or community advocates. Decisions require approval across multiple stakeholder classes, preventing any single group from dominating.

Algorithmic transparency mandates are a defining feature. Platform cooperatives require that the algorithms governing the platform — ranking, recommendation, content moderation, pricing — are transparent and subject to cooperative governance. This addresses the "black box" problem of traditional platforms, where opaque algorithms shape user experience and can be weaponized for profit extraction.

Some implementations go further, proposing platforms as "public infrastructure" — governed like utilities rather than companies, with regulatory obligations to serve the public interest. This model draws on historical precedents like public libraries, municipal utilities, and cooperative banks.

In the web3 context, platform cooperatives offer an alternative to the DAO model's heavy reliance on token-based governance. By incorporating formal stakeholder representation and transparency requirements, they create governance structures that are more accountable to the communities they serve.

## Problems Addressed

- [[Governance Theater]] — Multi-stakeholder governance with formal representation prevents the common DAO pattern where governance exists in name only while a small group makes actual decisions
- [[Technical and Legal Gaps]] — Platform cooperatives draw on established cooperative law and governance frameworks, providing legal clarity that many DAOs lack
- [[Lack of Clear Purpose]] — The cooperative structure explicitly defines the platform's purpose as serving its stakeholders and the public, rather than leaving purpose ambiguous

## Maturity Assessment

At Genesis-to-Custom (ex 2), platform cooperatives benefit from a long history of cooperative governance in other domains (agricultural, financial, housing) but are still being adapted for digital platforms and web3 contexts. The cooperative legal frameworks exist in many jurisdictions, providing a more solid legal foundation than most DAO structures. However, integrating cooperative governance with on-chain mechanisms and making it work at the scale of global digital platforms remains an open design challenge. The Public AI Network's focus on cooperative governance for AI represents a particularly timely application.
