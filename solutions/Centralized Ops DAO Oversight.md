> [!abstract] Alternative Models · Product
> 5 participant mentions · Addresses 3 governance problems

A hybrid model where foundations or core teams handle day-to-day operations while the DAO retains oversight over budgets, strategy, and key personnel decisions. Centralized Ops DAO Oversight acknowledges that full decentralization of operational decisions creates governance theater, and proposes a pragmatic division of labor between centralized execution and decentralized oversight.

## How It Works

The core insight is a separation between operational decisions and strategic governance. Operational decisions — hiring specific contractors, managing sprint priorities, deploying code updates, responding to security incidents — require speed, context, and accountability that decentralized voting cannot provide. Strategic decisions — setting annual budgets, approving major initiatives, selecting key leadership, defining organizational values — benefit from broad community input and democratic legitimacy.

In this model, a foundation, core team, or elected executive body handles day-to-day operations with significant autonomy. They make decisions quickly, take accountability for outcomes, and manage the organization's daily functions. The DAO community, meanwhile, retains oversight authority over the big decisions: approving the annual budget, ratifying the strategic plan, hiring and firing executive leadership, and setting policy constraints within which the operational team works.

The oversight mechanisms typically include regular reporting requirements (the operational team must publish financial reports, progress updates, and performance metrics), budget approval cycles (the DAO votes on annual or quarterly budgets), and recall provisions (the community can replace the operational team through a governance vote if performance is unsatisfactory).

This model draws explicitly from corporate governance, where boards of directors provide oversight without managing daily operations, and from public sector governance, where elected officials set policy while civil servants implement it. The adaptation for DAOs involves making the oversight mechanisms transparent, on-chain, and accessible to all token holders rather than a small board.

Critics argue this model sacrifices decentralization. Proponents counter that it's more honest — many DAOs already operate this way informally, with core teams making most decisions and the DAO rubber-stamping them. Formalizing the arrangement creates accountability where the informal version has none.

## Problems Addressed

- [[Governance Theater]] — Eliminates the pretense of community governance over operational decisions that are actually made by core teams, replacing theater with honest, accountable centralized operations and genuine community oversight
- [[Lack of Clear Purpose]] — Clearly delineates what the DAO governs (strategy, budgets, leadership) versus what operations handles (execution, daily management), creating clarity that reduces confusion and conflict
- [[Voting Fatigue]] — Dramatically reduces the number of decisions that require community voting, focusing governance attention on the strategic decisions that actually benefit from broad participation

## Key Actors

- **a16z (concept)** — Published influential frameworks arguing for the hybrid model, drawing on their portfolio experience with organizations that struggled under full decentralization
- **Various L2s** — Multiple Layer 2 networks effectively operate with this model, maintaining foundations or labs that handle operations while community governance provides oversight on strategic questions

## Maturity Assessment

At Product stage (ex 3.5), Centralized Ops DAO Oversight is one of the most widely practiced governance models — though it's often implemented informally rather than formally. Many major DAOs have foundations or core teams that handle operations while governance focuses on budgets and strategy. The formal version, with explicit accountability mechanisms and clear authority boundaries, is less common but growing. The model hasn't reached Commodity status because the exact division of authority between ops and governance is still being negotiated across organizations, and the community resistance to acknowledging centralization remains a political challenge.

## Participant Mentions

Referenced in 5 out of 30 interviews. The high mention count reflects both the pragmatic orientation of many governance practitioners and the widespread recognition that pure decentralization of operations produces poor outcomes. Participants who mentioned this model often did so with a note of pragmatic realism — they had seen enough governance dysfunction to value effective execution over ideological purity. Several noted that the key challenge is not the model itself but getting communities to accept it without feeling that decentralization has been abandoned.
